From mines to runways

The story of the world's favorite pants

Few garments have traveled as far as the blue jean. From the dusty gold mines of 1850s California to the catwalks of Paris, the high schools of Tokyo, and the streets of every city on earth. This is the story of how a pair of work trousers became the most worn piece of clothing in human history.

The Fabric that started it all: What Is Denim?

Denim takes its name from the French city of Nîmes, where weavers produced a tough, twill-woven cotton fabric known as serge de Nîmes, literally "fabric from Nîmes." By the 17th century, this sturdy cloth had made its way across Europe and eventually to the United States, where it would find its true calling. Unlike ordinary cotton, denim is woven in a diagonal ribbed pattern that makes it exceptionally durable and resistant to tearing, exactly what hard labour demands. The characteristic indigo blue colour came from dyeing only the warp threads, leaving the weft white, which is why the inside of jeans is always lighter than the outside.

Levi Strauss and the Gold Rush: The Birth of the Blue Jean

In 1853, a Bavarian immigrant named Levi Strauss arrived in San Francisco during the California Gold Rush, not to mine for gold, but to sell dry goods to the miners who did. Recognising that workers needed clothing that could withstand brutal conditions, Strauss partnered with tailor Jacob Davis in 1873 to patent a revolutionary idea: copper rivets at the stress points of trousers, the pocket corners and the base of the button fly. These riveted "waist overalls," made from that durable denim fabric, were an instant hit. Workers could finally wear pants that lasted longer than a few weeks in the field.

The Cultural Shift: Hollywood, Rebellion, and the 1950s

Everything changed in the 1950s. When Marlon Brando wore jeans in The Wild One (1953) and James Dean made them iconic in Rebel Without a Cause (1955), jeans stopped being workwear and became a statement. Young people across America adopted them as a uniform of rebellion against the conservative postwar establishment. Schools began banning jeans as inappropriate. Parents forbade them. That, of course, only made teenagers want them more. The garment that had clothed labourers for eighty years was suddenly the most powerful symbol of youth and counterculture the world had ever seen.
